Two cooled turbine vanes, measured in 1983 and solved today

forge couples its gas-side solution to a two-dimensional solid with ten cooling passages, and is checked here against the two vanes of NASA CR-168015. The report is organised one vane at a time: everything about C3X is in section 02, everything about Mark II in section 03, each ending with the same control — the turbulence model switched off — which shows the measurement sitting between the laminar and turbulent limits. Section 07 then adds a transition model, checks it on a flat plate and against SU2, and reports what it does and does not fix on the two vanes.

The wall heat flux that is reported

Every heat flux on this page is the same quantity: the heat the fluid gives up to the wall, per unit area, positive into the metal (iface_q_eff in the wall dump). That is not simply the flux the solver assembled on the wall face. At a wall node the energy equation is not solved — it is replaced by the imposed temperature — so the heat that reaches the wall is whatever the rest of the wall node's control volume delivers to it. Write the energy residual of that control volume as

Rraw = I + Fw,

where I is the net energy arriving through the faces it shares with the fluid and Fw is the contribution of the wall face (negative when heat leaves through it). Imposing the temperature supplies a reaction C = −Rraw that holds the node there, so the heat handed to whatever holds the wall at temperature is −Fw − C = I. That reaction is a discrete heat bath, and in the coupled problem the solid is that heat bath; where the measured wall temperature is imposed instead, the real metal and coolant were, and the report's own h was reduced from exactly that balance.

One correction is applied to I. These runs do not reach a steady state, and while the solution is still moving the wall control volume gains and loses a little mass. The energy that mass carries stays in the control volume; it has not gone to the metal. The volume is fixed, at rest and at the wall temperature, so what it stores is the internal energy ew per unit mass, and

A·q = I − ewRρ,

with Rρ the mass residual of the wall node. At a steady state Rρ is zero and the correction vanishes. Section 04 shows where it matters, which is at the foot of the C3X shock and nowhere else.

How this differs from the flux through the wall face

The reported flux is 2.8 % above the flux through the wall face alone on C3X and 3.2 % on Mark II. That difference is physical and its origin is known: it is the viscous work done inside the half control volume at the wall, the wall shear stress times the velocity half a cell out. It scales with the height of the first cell — 2.81 % at 2 µm and 1.42 % at 1 µm on C3X — and not with the mesh along the wall (3.3 / 3.2 / 3.5 % at 340 / 480 / 680 wall nodes on Mark II) or with where the 480 nodes sit (2.85 % on both C3X node placements). On the one-dimensional conduction slab, which has no flow, it is 5×10⁻⁶. SU2's surface heat flux is the wall-face estimate and does not contain this term, which is why section 02 also compares the two codes on that estimate.

What remains provisional is convergence, not the definition. No vane run on this page passes check_convergence.py; every one returns NOT CONVERGED (stalled/plateau) at about 2.4 decades. The balance above holds node by node whatever the state of the run. What an unconverged run leaves open is the state itself — the free cells away from the wall are not in balance — so each quoted number is taken from a run in which that number has stopped moving, judged quantity by quantity (end of this section).

How convergence is judged here

The residual gate stops being informative on these cases at about 2.4 decades, and it says the same thing at step 16 000 as at step 200 000. The answer does not. With SU2's turbulence settings the C3X pressure-surface bias climbed +11.2 → +14.4 → +15.9 → +16.1 → +16.2 % over steps 2000 / 20 000 / 40 000 / 60 000 / 200 000 and only then stopped moving. There is a slow mode the plateaued residual cannot see, so the judgment is made on each reported quantity instead: its history over the run's snapshots goes through check_quasisteady.py, and only quantities it returns as STEADY are quoted as values.

The production runs were taken to 200 000 steps (C3X) and 300 000 steps (Mark II) until flat, and then continued for 60 000 more after the face-weight correction of section 04. That correction moved the C3X suction-side laminar figure from +41.5 to +44.6 % within the first 5000 steps; from step 15 000 to 60 000 every regional figure on both vanes moves by less than 0.1 point, except Mark II after transition, which wanders by ±0.3.

The measured quantity does nothing and the unmeasured one does everything. With the viscosity ratio held at 10, taking Tu from 3 % to 15 % — a factor of 25 in the inlet turbulent kinetic energy (11.8 to 296 m²/s², with ω scaled to keep the ratio, around the 55.6 m²/s² and 2.17×10⁵ s⁻¹ used), far outside the ±10 % the report claims — moves the pressure surface by 0.3 points and the post-transition suction side by 0.1. Changing μt/μ from 1 to 100 moves the pressure surface by 23 points and the laminar stretch by 16. So tuning the inlet turbulence would work, and should not be done: the lever is a parameter the experiment never constrained, and it does not reach the error that matters — at μt/μ = 1 the laminar stretch is still +42.5 %, and the post-transition side stays within 0.8 points across the whole factor of 100.

Over the range chosen here, then, the pressure-surface figure moves by about −5 / +18 points with a number the experiment does not fix. That is a sensitivity over a chosen range on the uniform-wall case, not an uncertainty budget, and it is not carried over to the measured-wall figures above.

And how much of it is the missing transition

Every result above points the same way: the suction surface is laminar in the experiment and turbulent in the model. One more sensitivity test bears on that — take the turbulent solution, switch the turbulence model off so the flow is laminar everywhere, change nothing else, and run it out. It brackets the measurement; it does not place transition, which would take a computation with the transition location controlled.

Coupled: the metal temperature as a result

The loop is: run the fluid with a wall temperature, take the heat the fluid gives up at each of the 480 wall nodes, solve two-dimensional conduction in the metal cross-section with that heat on the outside and the coolant on the inside, hand the resulting outside temperature back to the fluid, repeat. The metal is a finite-element mesh of the actual section (7438 nodes) whose outer nodes coincide with the fluid wall nodes; each of the ten holes is a convective boundary with one heat transfer coefficient and one coolant temperature. Both come from the report and neither is fitted: the coolant temperature and flow rate of each hole are tabulated in Appendix A, and the report states the correlation it used, NuD = Cr·0.022·Pr0.5ReD0.8, which gives 1380 to 2670 W/m²K here.

Two errors in earlier versions of this page, both on the coupled side

The cooling holes were in the wrong place. The report gives the hole centres in its own (U, V) frame and does not say how that frame sits on the vane. They had been placed by searching for the rigid motion that kept every hole inside the metal with the largest minimum wall. Read from the report's figure, the frame is simply the tangent line along the pressure side and the perpendicular tangent at the leading edge; in that frame the vane measures 14.54 cm against the report's true chord of 14.49. The earlier placement was 7.4 mm too far forward, half a hole spacing (2.3 mm on Mark II). With the holes misplaced, the report's own coolant conditions reproduced the measured wall temperature only to 60 K rms, which had been taken as a reason to fit a coefficient per hole instead; the fit returned values from 280 to 9000 W/m²K. With the holes where the report puts them, the unfitted conditions reproduce it to 17 K, and a fit, run only as a check, returns 1270 to 2830 — within about ten percent of the correlation for the nine holes the data constrain.

The coupling loop stopped short of its answer. It accepted an update only if it lowered the interface imbalance and doubled its damping at every rejection. The fluid's answer to a new wall temperature is not complete after one 4000-step evaluation, so steps in the right direction were judged worse, and the wall temperature froze about 5 K low while the change per iteration went to zero. The loop now averages the interface flux over the last four snapshots of each evaluation, keeps rejection only as a guard against divergence, and stops when the mean wall temperature has stopped drifting (below 0.002 K per iteration over six iterations) with the imbalance no longer falling.

The regional pattern of the metal temperature follows that of the gas side: smallest where h is closest (+6.5 %, +6.4 K) and largest where it is furthest (+44.1 %, +21.1 K). With nothing fitted on the coolant side this is now a prediction rather than a consistency check, but it still does not separate gas-side from solid-side error: imposing the measured heat flux on this solid leaves 17 K rms of its own (mostly a −10 K bias), and neither the solid mesh nor the metal conductivity has been varied on the corrected model.

The trailing edge was wrong, and it did not matter here

The base cut described in section 01 was rounded on every Mark II run before the production one. Correcting it and rerunning with nothing else changed moves the regional biases by +0.8 on the pressure surface, −0.5 on the laminar stretch and −0.7 after transition — the last inside the ±1 point that quantity oscillates by anyway. A 0.71 mm geometry error at the trailing edge of a vane with a supersonic exit changes the heat transfer coefficient over s/S ≤ 0.87 by less than a point. Worth knowing in both directions: the shape had to be fixed, and fixing it rescued nothing. (These are differences between two runs made before the face-weight correction, which is common to both.)

04 / Wall heat fluxWhy the curves were ragged, and what fixed it

In earlier versions of this page the heat flux curves were visibly ragged while the pressure curves were not — same wall, same nodes, same solution. Five causes were found. The first two are defects in the solver and account for nearly all of it; they were found last, by writing out the energy balance of the wall control volume term by term, and they are corrected in every result on this page. The other three are about the mesh, the reconstruction and the boundary data.

Two measures are used below, both on the suction surface between s/S 0.45 and 0.95. The wave amplitude is the rms of the flux minus its 41-node moving mean. The two-node amplitude is the rms of ai = (qi − (qi−1 + qi+1)/2)/2, as a percentage of the mean flux; for a pure zig-zag qi = q̄ + a(−1)i it returns a, so neighbouring nodes differ by twice that figure.

First, the weight used to interpolate to a face. Between a wall node and the first node off the wall there is one face, and the viscous work crossing it is the shear stress times the velocity at that face. The wall node is at rest, so the face velocity is (1 − f)·U1, where f is the interpolation weight toward the wall node. In an edge-based scheme the face sits at the middle of the edge and f is one half. forge instead computed f from the mesh — the distance from the face centroid to each of the two nodes — on the assumption that this comes out at one half anyway. On a wall-resolving mesh around a curved vane it does not, for two reasons.

  • The distance was measured in a way that depends on how the wall is turned relative to the coordinate axes. The distance of the face centroid from a node, along the face normal n, is the dot product n·Δ = nxΔx + nyΔy. The code multiplied the two vectors component by component and took the length of the result, √((nxΔx)² + (nyΔy)²). Split the offset into a part b along the normal and a part a along the wall. The dot product returns b and ignores a, as it should. The other form returns √(b²(nx⁴+ny⁴) + 2ab·nxny(ny²−nx²) + 2a²nx²ny²): it depends on a, and its middle term changes sign between the two nodes of the edge, so the two distances come out unequal even when the face is exactly midway. With a = b and the face midway, the weight is 0.50 on a wall at 0°, 45° or 90° to the x axis, 0.37 at 22.5° and 0.63 at 67.5°. Wherever the node spacing along the wall changes, the face centroid is displaced along the wall by about a micron, and half the first cell is one micron, so a and b really are the same size here. A flat plate along the x axis is blind to this, which is why the plate verification never showed it.
  • Even measured correctly, the geometric weight is not one half on a curved wall. The neighbouring wall nodes lie below the tangent at a node by the sag of the chord, κΔs²/8 — 0.1 to 1 µm on C3X, again the size of the first cell — and that pulls the face centroid toward the wall.

The weight toward the wall node came out between 0.07 and 0.96 around the vane, and between 0.42 and 0.75 over the rear suction surface in a pattern some 25 nodes long — fixed in space, because it is a property of the mesh. Evaluating the code's formula outside the solver reproduces the solver's own viscous-work term node by node to 0.0 W/m²; the correct dot product does not (correlation 0.14).

Rotating the flat-plate problem by 30° — mesh, solution and inlet velocity together, which should change nothing — moved the wall shear stress by 0.085 % and the wall-face heat flux by 0.25 % with the old weight, and by 0.024 % and 0.06 % with the new one, compared after 4000 steps. Those two runs are not converged, so what remains has not been attributed; the rounding of the rotated single-precision coordinates is a candidate. Rerun from a cold start, the cooled flat-plate verification reproduces its reference to 0.2 % in friction and heat flux.

Second, mass moving in and out of the wall control volume. With the weight corrected, a two-node spike of +5 / −6.5 kW/m² was still left at the foot of the shock. It is in the convective term. The residual of these runs stalls, and the shock foot keeps shifting by a fraction of a cell as the iteration goes on; while it does, the wall control volumes under it gain and lose mass, and the energy carried by that mass was being counted as heat given up to the wall. The convective part of the wall energy balance follows the mass imbalance of the same node with a correlation of 1.000 and a slope of 571 kJ/kg, the wall enthalpy cpTw = 568.5.

Energy that accumulates in the control volume has not gone to the metal, so it is now subtracted (section 01). What is subtracted is the internal energy ew per unit of accumulated mass, not the enthalpy Hw the inflow carries: the difference, p/ρ per unit mass, is the work of pushing mass into a fixed volume held at constant temperature, and that does leave as heat. The first version of this correction subtracted the enthalpy; an independent review caught it. At a steady state the mass imbalance is zero and nothing changes — on the conduction slab the reported flux moved from −81.5088 to −81.5132 W/m² against a wall-face estimate of −81.5131.

Third, where the wall nodes sit. The spacing of the wall nodes alternated by 2.8 % from one node to the next, because the outline resampler weights point density by curvature and that curvature estimate is itself noisy. Smoothing the curvature estimate — not the spacing, which would strip the trailing edge — takes the alternation to 0.31 % on the production meshes and 0.026 % on the mesh used for the SU2 comparison. It is not a refinement: the same 480 wall nodes on the same curve, in different places.

Fourth, the reconstruction. SU2 reconstructs temperature and derives density; forge reconstructed density and derived temperature, which made T the difference of two independent reconstructions and gave it the noise of both. Reconstructing T instead, with a limiter built from its own extrema, halved the node-to-node alternation of the wall flux in the runs where it was tested (0.048 ± 0.002 to 0.021 ± 0.016 %, measured then as the mean alternating difference between neighbours over s/S 0.30–0.80). It is on in every run on this page.

Fifth, the boundary data. The production runs impose 68 thermocouple readings joined by straight lines across 480 nodes. Every kink of that polyline is a step in wall temperature gradient, and the boundary layer answers each one; that response is what is still visible in Figures 6 and 15, and it is a property of the data as imposed, not of the solver — with a uniform wall the same mesh gives the smooth black curve of Figure 18. Smoothing the imposed temperature was rejected: the choice of interpolant alone moves the distribution by up to 7.6 K on C3X and 11.5 K on Mark II, and nothing in the report constrains the shape between thermocouples.

Before the term-by-term balance pointed at the face weight, single precision, the turbulence model, the molecular transport law, the diagonal of the implicit solve and a contact-wave floor in the convective flux were each tried as the cause. None of them moved the measure it was tested on, and none is switched on or changed as a result; the records are in the case README.

05 / ConvergenceRuns that stall, and how they are read

No vane run on this page passes check_convergence.py; all of them stall at about 2.4 decades. One candidate for the cause has been observed directly: a laminar dual-time run of C3X sheds from its 1.7 mm trailing edge at 29 kHz, and halving the physical time step reproduces the frequency, so in that run it is physical. That the stall of the steady SST runs is the same shedding has not been shown — it would take a dual-time run of the production configuration, with the reported quantities averaged in time — and section 04 adds a second unsteady spot, the foot of the C3X shock.

The consequence carried through the page is that judgment moves from the residual to the reported quantity: each number comes from a run in which that number has stopped moving (the table at the end of section 01), and where it has not, the page says so and gives a range or nothing. The residual said "as converged as it will get" 140 000 steps before the C3X pressure-surface figure stopped moving.

06 / Also fixedThree more defects in the solver itself

Besides the face weight and the accumulation term of section 04. Each was reproduced, fixed and regression-checked.

  • The wall diagnostics were all zero on a resolved wall. At wallTreatment 0 with no energy wall function, nobody wrote qwall or uτ, so the wall dump reported zero heat flux and zero friction velocity for every low-Reynolds run. They now carry the values actually put into the residual, with the same sign convention as the model path.
  • The inlet corner node fought the wall. Where the inlet meets the vane wall, two boundary conditions claimed the same node and the pressure ran away. mesh.nodeInletCornerWall resolves it at conversion time.
  • The coupling loop carried the wrong flux by default. Its default was the first-interior-point estimate while the design note called for the heat the wall control volume actually exchanged. Both defaults are now the conservative one; recoupling both vanes moved the metal by one to two kelvin, in opposite directions on the two vanes.

07 / Transition modelAdding the missing closure: what it fixes, and what it does not

Sections 02 and 03 end on the same finding: the turbulence model is turbulent from the leading edge, the measured boundary layer is not, and the front of the suction surface is over-predicted by 44 to 80 %. This section adds the closure that was missing and reports what happened. The short version: on a flat plate it reproduces the measured transition and matches an independent code to within a percent; on the vanes it removes the over-prediction it was brought in for, but it transitions later and more abruptly than the measurement, it leaves the pressure surface too cold, and how well the whole vane agrees depends on an inlet quantity the 1983 report does not give.

What was added

The model is the two-equation correlation model of Langtry and Menter (2009). It carries two more quantities with the flow. The first, the intermittency, is the fraction of the time the boundary layer at that point is turbulent: 1 in the free stream, close to 0 inside a laminar layer. It multiplies the production of turbulent kinetic energy in the SST model, so where it is small the layer stays laminar. The second carries, from the free stream into the boundary layer, the Reynolds number at which transition should start; that threshold comes from an empirical correlation in the local turbulence level and the local acceleration of the flow. Transition starts where the boundary layer has grown past the threshold. A separate term switches the layer turbulent quickly if it separates while still laminar.

The implementation follows the one in SU2 8.5 equation by equation, so that the two codes can be compared on identical meshes. It is off unless requested, and with it off the solver gives the same fields as before to within the scatter between two runs of the old binary (forge does not reproduce itself bit for bit from run to run, so the comparison is against that scatter: the largest difference is 1.8 times it, within the factor of two allowed). Each node of the solver's source terms was checked against a separate double-precision implementation of the same equations, on the flat plate and on the C3X passage: all eleven quantities agree at every node.

  • What it was brought in for, it does. The front of the suction surface goes from +46 to +59 % down to −3 to −20 %, and up to s/S = 0.15 the computed curve runs through the measured points.
  • The transition is too late and too abrupt. The measured heat transfer rises gradually from s/S = 0.2 to 0.4. The computed layer stays laminar to 0.3, by which point the heat transfer has fallen to a fifth of the measured value, and then jumps to the turbulent level within 0.04 of the surface length and overshoots by 18 %. This is why the rms after transition gets worse (12.8 % without the model, 30 to 36 % with it) although the mean looks good. The location hardly moves with the inlet setting (0.32 to 0.34 for ratios of 10, 30 and 100). The cause is visible in the model's own variables: the suction-side flow accelerates to five times the inlet velocity by s/S = 0.2, the model measures turbulence intensity against the local velocity, so the 6.5 % at the inlet is about 1 % there and the threshold stays out of reach until the flow starts to decelerate at 0.3; the jump that follows is the separated-layer term. In the measurement transition begins where the acceleration ends.
  • The pressure surface comes out too cold, and that is only half wrong. The flow along it accelerates strongly: the acceleration parameter K = (ν/U²) dU/ds is above 3 × 10⁻⁶, the level at which a turbulent layer cannot be sustained, up to mid-surface (8.3, 4.1 and 2.9 × 10⁻⁶ at s/S = 0.1, 0.3 and 0.5). A laminar front half is therefore what one expects, and the measurement is low there. But the measured level is still 25 to 80 % above the computed laminar layer — a laminar layer buffeted by 6.5 % turbulence — and rises towards the turbulent level over the rear half, once K falls below 3. The model has no mechanism for the first and does not produce the second: with inlet ratios of 1, 10 and 30 the pressure surface stays laminar to the trailing edge. At a ratio of 100 it reads +25 %, but not because the layer has transitioned (the eddy viscosity within 0.1 mm of the wall stays small up to s/S = 0.76); the free stream then carries so much eddy viscosity that it diffuses into the thin layer.
  • No single number says whether the vane as a whole got better. Compared like for like, the model improves every region at an inlet ratio of 100 (rms 43 to 29 %) and improves nothing overall at 10 (34 to 37 %, with the sign reversed). The report gives the turbulence intensity at the inlet and not its length scale, so there is no basis for choosing between them, and none has been chosen. Limiting the production of turbulence at the stagnation point (Kato–Launder), which the model's authors recommend, moves the pressure surface at a ratio of 100 from +25 to −26 %; which of the two is right is not known either.
  • These runs have to be long. At a ratio of 30 the pressure-surface figure read +18 % after 60 000 steps and settled at −22 % after 200 000, unchanged to 500 000.

Is that the model or the solver? The same model in SU2

They agree. Region by region the heat transfer coefficient differs by −0.4 % on the pressure surface, +0.1 % on the front of the suction surface and +1.4 % behind it, and the rise through transition crosses its mid-point at s/S = 0.337 in both codes. SU2 is as late, as abrupt and as cold on the pressure surface as forge (−30.7 against −30.9 % there). For this mesh, wall condition and inlet setting, then, what the previous subsection describes is not particular to forge's implementation. It is one condition and one final field, so it does not by itself establish that the model behaves this way in general. (This mesh has a 2 µm first cell and fails the wall-resolution check, as in section 02; the comparison is between codes, not with the measurement. 09 / OpenWhat is not settled

  • The transition closure is now there, and it is not enough. Section 07: the model removes the over-prediction on the front of the suction surface and matches SU2 to a percent, but on C3X it transitions at s/S = 0.32 where the measurement starts at 0.2, it cannot represent a laminar layer heated by free-stream turbulence, and it does not transition the rear of the pressure surface. Whether the vane as a whole agrees better depends on the decay rate of the inlet turbulence, which the report does not give. The coupled metal temperature has not been recomputed with it.
  • The state the numbers are taken from. Every vane run stalls at about 2.4 decades. The quoted quantities are steady by their own histories, but no vane configuration here has a residual that actually falls, and the foot of the C3X shock keeps moving. A dual-time run of the production configuration, with time-averaged heat flux, is the missing check.
  • Wall-normal resolution. The 2 µm production meshes fail the y1⁺ ≤ 1 gate on two thirds of the wall. On C3X the 1 µm mesh passes and moves the figures by 1.4 to 2.3 points. Mark II at 1 µm (section 07) moves them by 1.0 to 2.7 points and still fails without the transition model (11 % of the wall above 1); its post-transition figure is also still rising with refinement along the wall.
  • The viscous work at the wall is only first-order accurate in relative terms. A manufactured-solution study on a curved, stretched, high-aspect wall layer (the face formula checked against the solver's own kernel to 0.3 W/m²) shows the corrected weight giving second-order temperature and velocity. The viscous work entering the wall control volume converges too, but because the work itself shrinks with the first cell, its relative error falls only at first order, from 31 % on the coarsest grid: on a curved wall the edge midpoint where the face velocity is taken and the broken dual face over which the exact flux is integrated are not in the same place. That term is 2.8 % of the reported flux at the C3X resolution, so the effect on the flux is below a percent and halves with the first cell; its sign and size on the real vane have not been evaluated. With the old weight the same relative error stayed near 25 % at every refinement. SST and species diffusion were not part of that study.
  • The remaining forge–SU2 difference, 2.6 % on the suction side and 2.8 % on the pressure side when both are evaluated as the wall-face estimate. Its origin is not identified.
  • The pressure deficit toward the trailing edge, identical in both codes. The two-dimensional idealisation is a candidate, not a demonstrated cause; the report does not publish the axial velocity density ratio.
  • The cost and the noise of the coupling loop. It now converges in one pass, but takes 30 to 70 fluid evaluations, and on Mark II the interface imbalance stays near 0.1 % of the heat load because the fluid flux itself is noisy. Coupling inside the solver, so the wall temperature moves with the flow instead of between evaluations, is the way to remove both.
  • The solid side on its own. With the measured heat flux imposed, the conduction model leaves 16 to 17 K rms against the measured wall temperature, mostly a bias of −7 to −10 K. The metal conductivity is from an outside source (the report gives no value), the last hole lies beyond the measured range, and neither the solid mesh nor the conductivity has been varied on the corrected model.
  • Two configuration keys that are silently ignored. turbulence.kInf and omegaInf appear nowhere in the solver; the inlet turbulence comes from the boundary condition file alone. No number on this page was affected, but a sweep of the wrong key returned four bit-identical runs before it was noticed. The solver should reject unknown keys.
